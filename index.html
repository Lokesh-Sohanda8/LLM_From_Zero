Lesson 01

The Family Tree of an LLM

An LLM isn’t one invention — it’s the newest ring in a decades-old tree of research, each ring narrowing the focus of the one before it.

Artificial Intelligence — machines that act smart
Machine Learning — learns from data
Deep Learning — learns with neural networks
NLP — understands language
Transformers — attention-based architecture
LLMyou are here

Every ring here is a specialization of the ring around it. AI is the whole field of making machines behave intelligently. Machine Learning is the part of AI that learns patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules. Deep Learning is ML done with layered neural networks. NLP applies deep learning to language specifically. Transformers are the architecture that made modern NLP possible. And an LLM is simply a very large Transformer, trained on a huge amount of text.

💡
Think of it like: a set of Russian nesting dolls — you can’t understand the smallest doll without knowing it lives inside all the bigger ones.
Lesson 02

What Is Machine Learning?

Instead of programming exact rules, we show the machine examples — and let it work out the pattern itself.

Training Datapast examples
Featuresthe useful signals
ML Modelguesses a rule
Prediction
measure
Errorhow wrong it was
↻ repeats thousands of times, adjusting the model a little each round

A machine learning model starts out knowing nothing. You show it data, it makes a guess, you tell it how wrong the guess was (the error), and it nudges its internal numbers to be a little less wrong next time. Do this enough times on enough examples, and the model converges on a rule that generalizes to data it has never seen.

💡
Think of it like: teaching someone to throw darts blindfolded — they throw, you tell them ‘too far left,’ they adjust, and over hundreds of throws they get accurate without ever seeing the board.
Lesson 03

Neural Networks: Stacking Simple Math

A neural network is just several simple transformations stacked on top of each other, layer by layer.

Inputraw numbers
Hidden Layer 1
Hidden Layer 2
Hidden Layer 3
Outputthe answer

Each layer takes the numbers from the layer before it, multiplies them by learned weights, adds a bias, and passes the result through a small non-linear function. No single layer is smart on its own — but stack enough of them and the network can approximate extremely complex functions, like ‘is this a cat photo’ or ‘what word comes next.’

💡
Think of it like: an assembly line — each station does one small, simple operation, but after enough stations the raw material has become a finished product.
Lesson 04

How a Network Actually Learns

Training is a loop: guess, compare, measure how wrong, then fix the weights that caused the mistake.

Backpropagation is the key idea: once we know the loss (how wrong the final prediction was), we can mathematically trace back through every layer to figure out exactly how much each individual weight contributed to that error — and nudge each one in the direction that reduces it.

💡
Think of it like: a group project post-mortem — after a bad result, you trace back through every decision to see who contributed how much to the mistake, so each person knows exactly what to adjust next time.

Computers only understand numbers, but language is made of words, grammar, and context. 💡
Think of it like: a translator standing between two people who speak entirely different languages — one who speaks ‘words’ and one who only speaks ‘numbers.’
Lesson 06

Tokenization: Chopping Text into Pieces

Before a model can process text, it has to be split into small, consistent chunks called tokens.

“I love artificial intelligence”
tokenizer
Iloveartificialintelligence

A tokenizer doesn’t always split on whole words — common words often stay whole, while rare or long words get chopped into familiar sub-pieces (like art + ificial). This lets the model represent literally any word, even ones it has never seen, using a fixed, manageable vocabulary of pieces.

💡
Think of it like: cutting a sentence into LEGO bricks — common shapes stay as one big brick, unusual shapes get broken into several small bricks the model already recognizes.
Lesson 07

From Tokens to Token IDs

Every token in the model’s vocabulary has a fixed integer ID — a lookup, nothing more.

cat
4021
dog
4022
runs
8710

The model’s vocabulary (often 50,000–150,000 tokens) is just a giant lookup table decided once, before training. “cat” is always ID 4021 for that model. This turns text into a simple list of integers — the only format a neural network actually understands.

💡
Think of it like: a coat-check counter — you hand in your word, you get back a numbered ticket, and that number is all the system needs to remember what you handed in.
Lesson 08

Embeddings: Giving Numbers Meaning

A token ID alone carries no meaning — embeddings convert it into a rich vector that captures meaning.

Token IDe.g. 4021
Embedding Layerlearned lookup table
High-Dim Vectore.g. 768 numbers
Semantic Representation

ID 4021 tells the model nothing about what “cat” means — it’s an arbitrary label. The embedding layer maps that ID to a vector of hundreds of numbers, learned during training so that words with related meanings end up with similar vectors. This is where a token stops being a symbol and starts being a point in ‘meaning space.’

💡
Think of it like: translating a word into GPS coordinates on a map of meaning — the number itself looks arbitrary, but its position relative to other words is not.
Lesson 09

Semantic Vector Space

Words with related meaning land near each other in vector space — distance encodes similarity.

animals cat dog wolf
vehicles car truck

Plot every word’s embedding vector (simplified here to 2 dimensions) and a pattern appears: cat, dog, and wolf cluster together, far from car and truck, which cluster together too. Nobody told the model animals and vehicles are different categories — it discovered that structure purely from how these words are used in text.

💡
Think of it like: a well-organized library where books get shelved by topic automatically — you never told the librarian ‘animals go together,’ but that’s where you find them.
Lesson 10

Positional Information: Word Order Matters

“Dog bites man” and “man bites dog” use the same words — order changes everything.

The+ pos 1
dog+ pos 2
chased+ pos 3
the+ pos 4
cat+ pos 5

Embeddings alone tell the model what each word means, but not where it sits in the sentence — and a Transformer looks at all tokens at once, not in reading order. So a positional signal is mathematically added to every token’s embedding, letting the model tell “dog chased cat” apart from “cat chased dog.”

💡
Think of it like: stamping every word with a timestamp before throwing all the words into a bag — you can dump them out in any order and still reconstruct the original sentence.
Lesson 11

Why Attention? Context Changes Meaning

The word “it” is meaningless on its own — its meaning depends entirely on the rest of the sentence.

The animal didn’t cross the road because it was too tired.

“it” → animal  ✓
“it” → road  ✗

A model reading this sentence has to figure out that “it” refers to the animal, not the road — because “tired” makes sense for a living thing, not a road. This kind of reasoning requires every word to be able to look at every other word and weigh how relevant it is. That mechanism is called attention, and it’s the single most important idea behind modern LLMs.

💡
Think of it like: a detective re-reading every earlier clue in a story before deciding what a pronoun really refers to.
Lesson 12

Self-Attention: Every Token Looks at Every Token

Self-attention lets each token gather context from every other token in the sequence, weighted by relevance.

All Token Reps
Querywhat am I looking for?
Keyswhat do others offer?
Attention Scoresrelevance
Attention Weightsnormalized
Context-Aware Output

For every token, the model asks a question (Query), compares it against what every other token offers (Keys), and scores how relevant each one is. Those scores become weights, and the token’s new representation becomes a weighted blend of all the other tokens — now ‘aware’ of its context instead of standing alone.

💡
Think of it like: a group discussion where, before speaking, everyone briefly checks in with everyone else in the room to see whose input is most relevant to the current topic.
Lesson 13

Query, Key, Value — The Three Roles

Query asks “what am I looking for,” Key answers “here’s what I contain,” Value carries the actual content.

Input Representation
Query (Q)the question
Key (K)the label
Q &times; Kmatch score
Softmaxturn into weights
Weighted Values
Attention Output

Every token is projected into three different vectors using three separate learned weight matrices. The Query represents what this token is searching for. The Key represents what each token has to offer, and matching Q against K produces relevance scores. Those scores are used to blend the Values — the actual content each token carries.

💡
Think of it like: a library search: your Query is your search terms, every book’s Key is its catalog tags, and once you find good matches, the Value is the actual book content you walk away with.
Lesson 14

The Attention Formula, Step by Step

Behind the diagrams is one compact formula — here’s exactly what each piece does.

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax( QKT / √dk ) V
Q &times; Kᵀsimilarity scores
&divide; &radic;dₖkeep values stable
Softmaxturn into probabilities
&times; Vblend the values
Attention Output

QKT multiplies queries against keys to get raw similarity scores. Dividing by √dk (the square root of the key dimension) simply keeps those numbers from growing too large as vectors get bigger. Softmax turns the scores into a clean probability distribution that sums to 1. Multiplying by V then blends the actual content according to those probabilities.

💡
Think of it like: a recipe with exactly four steps — each one boring on its own, but skip any single step and the whole dish fails.
Lesson 15

Multi-Head Attention: Many Perspectives at Once

Instead of paying attention one way, the model runs several attention ‘heads’ in parallel, each free to learn a different kind of relationship.

Input
Head 1e.g. grammar
Head 2e.g. topic
Head 3e.g. tone
Head 4e.g. position
Concatenate
Linear Projection
Multi-Head Output

One attention head might learn to track grammatical structure, another might track which pronoun refers to which noun, another might track topic. Running several heads in parallel — then concatenating and projecting their outputs back down — lets the model capture many kinds of relationships simultaneously instead of being forced to pick just one.

💡
Think of it like: a panel of specialists reviewing the same document at once — a grammar expert, a fact-checker, a tone reader — then combining all their notes into one final review.
Lesson 16

The Feed-Forward Network

After attention mixes information between tokens, the feed-forward network processes each token individually to refine it.

Attention Output
Linear Layerexpand
Activationadd non-linearity
Linear Layercompress back
FFN Output

Attention is about tokens talking to each other; the FFN is the opposite — it takes each token’s (now context-aware) representation and pushes it through its own small neural network, independently of every other token, to further refine what it ‘knows.’ This combination of ‘mix, then think’ repeats throughout the whole model.

💡
Think of it like: a team meeting (attention, everyone shares notes) followed by quiet, independent desk work (FFN, each person processes what they just heard on their own).
Lesson 17

Residual Connections: Don’t Lose the Original

A shortcut path carries the original input forward so it never gets lost — only added to.

Input
Self-Attention
Add + Residual
Updated Representation
↪ the original Input is carried straight through and added back in, not replaced

Instead of letting a layer completely overwrite a token’s representation, the layer’s output is added to the original input via a shortcut connection. This means each layer only has to learn what to change, not how to reconstruct everything from scratch — which makes very deep networks (dozens of stacked layers) far easier to train.

💡
Think of it like: editing a document with tracked changes instead of retyping it from memory — you keep the original text and only layer your edits on top.
Lesson 18

One Transformer Block, Assembled

Attention, then a feed-forward network, each wrapped in normalization and a residual connection — this is the repeating unit of every LLM.

This is where every idea from the last few lessons comes together into one reusable unit: tokens exchange context through multi-head attention, get stabilized and combined with their original signal (add + norm), get individually refined by the FFN, and get stabilized again. This exact block is what actually gets stacked to build the full model.

💡
Think of it like: one lap on a factory conveyor belt — the product goes through the same two stations (mix, then refine) with a quality check after each one, and the whole belt just loops this lap over and over.
Lesson 19

GPT-3 has 96 of these blocks stacked; smaller models might have 12. Each block takes the previous block’s output and refines it a bit further — early blocks tend to capture simple patterns like grammar, while later blocks capture more abstract meaning and reasoning. Depth is one of the main reasons scale helps LLMs get smarter.

💡
Think of it like: sharpening a pencil — each pass of the blade removes a little more wood, and it takes many passes before you get a fine point.
Lesson 20

Encoder vs. Decoder

Transformers come in two flavors, depending on whether the goal is to understand text or to generate it.

Transformer
EncoderUnderstand / Represent<br><em>e.g. BERT</em>
DecoderGenerate Tokens<br><em>e.g. GPT</em>

An encoder looks at the whole input at once and builds a rich representation of it — good for classification, search, and understanding tasks. A decoder generates text one token at a time, only looking backward at what it has already produced — good for writing and conversation. Most modern chat-style LLMs (GPT, Claude, Llama) are decoder-only.

💡
Think of it like: an editor who reads an entire manuscript before summarizing it (encoder) versus a novelist writing one sentence at a time, never allowed to peek at pages they haven’t written yet (decoder).

If the model could peek at future tokens while learning to predict them, it would be cheating — and useless at generation time, since future tokens don’t exist yet when you’re writing them. The causal mask forces each token to attend only to itself and everything before it, which keeps training and real-world generation consistent.

💡
Think of it like: taking a closed-book exam one question at a time — you can use everything you’ve already answered, but the next question stays covered up until it’s your turn.

Lesson 23

Next-Token Prediction

An LLM doesn’t know the ‘right’ next word — it produces a probability for every word in its vocabulary.

“The sky is …”
LLM
blue

dark

clear

full of stars

Given “The sky is…”, the model doesn’t deterministically output “blue.” It computes a probability for every token in its vocabulary, and “blue” simply scores highest given everything it learned from training data. A sampling strategy then picks one token — often the highest-probability one, sometimes a slightly less likely one for variety.

💡
Think of it like: a weather forecaster who never says ‘it will rain’ — only ‘70% chance of rain, 20% cloudy, 10% clear,’ leaving the final call to how the forecast gets used.
Lesson 24

Autoregressive Generation

The model generates one token, appends it to its own input, and runs itself again — over and over.

I love
+ AI
I love AI
+ because
I love AI because
+ it
I love AI because it
+ helps
I love AI because it helps

“Autoregressive” just means the model feeds its own output back in as part of the next input. Every single word you see an LLM produce is really the entire prompt-so-far being re-processed from scratch through the whole Transformer stack, one more time, to predict just the next token.

💡
Think of it like: writing a story one word at a time, and before every new word, silently re-reading the entire story so far from the beginning.
Lesson 25

Training an LLM

The exact same predict-compare-adjust loop from Lesson 4 — just run on billions of sentences of real text.

Massive Text Dataset
Training Tokens
Transformer LLM
Predicted Token
Actual Token
Loss
Backpropagation
Update Weights
↻ repeated across trillions of tokens, for weeks on thousands of GPUs

For every sentence in a giant training set, the model is shown some words and asked to predict the next one. It compares its guess to the real next word, computes a loss, backpropagates that error through every layer, and updates its weights slightly. Scale that up to a trillion+ tokens of text and you get a model that has absorbed an enormous amount of world knowledge and language structure — purely as a side effect of getting better at next-token prediction.

💡
Think of it like: learning a language by reading an entire library, one sentence at a time, constantly quizzing yourself on ‘what word comes next’ and correcting your intuition after every guess.
Lesson 26

Pretraining → Fine-Tuning → Alignment

A raw pretrained model is a text predictor. Two more stages turn it into a helpful assistant.

Massive Raw Datasetinternet-scale text
Pretraininglearn language & facts
Base Modelpredicts text, but untamed
Instruction Datasetexample Q&amp;A pairs
Fine-Tuninglearn to follow instructions

Pretraining teaches the raw model language, facts, and reasoning patterns from huge amounts of text — but it just predicts plausible next text, with no sense of being ‘helpful’ or ‘safe.’ Fine-tuning shows it curated examples of instructions and good responses. Alignment then uses human preference rankings to steer it further toward answers people actually prefer — turning a raw text predictor into the assistant you chat with.

💡
Think of it like: raising someone through school (pretraining — broad general knowledge), then job training (fine-tuning — how to do this specific role), then on-the-job coaching based on feedback (alignment — how to do it the way people actually want).
Lesson 27

Training vs. Inference

Training is the one-time (expensive) process of learning weights. Inference is every single time you actually use the model.

TRAINING &mdash; happens once
Dataset
Loss
Backpropagation
Updated Weights
VS
INFERENCE &mdash; happens every chat
User Prompt
Fixed Weightsfrozen, unchanged
Forward Pass Only
Generated Answer

During training, the model’s weights are constantly changing as it learns from data — this happens once (or occasionally, for updates), and is extremely expensive. During inference — every time you send a message to an LLM — the weights are completely frozen. The model just runs a forward pass through fixed weights to produce a response; nothing about it is being ‘learned’ from your conversation.

💡
Think of it like: the difference between years spent training for a marathon (training, you change and improve) and actually running race day (inference, you just execute what you already learned — no more improving mid-race).
Lesson 28

RAG: Retrieval-Augmented Generation

An LLM only knows what was in its training data. RAG hands it fresh, specific facts to read before answering.

Instead of relying purely on what the model memorized during training (which can be outdated or simply not include your private data), RAG first searches a knowledge base for the most relevant documents, then stuffs those documents into the prompt as extra context. The LLM then answers using that fresh, specific information instead of guessing from memory.

💡
Think of it like: an open-book exam — instead of relying only on what you memorized, you’re allowed to quickly look up the exact page that answers the question before you write your response.
Lesson 29

The LLM itself never runs code or hits an API — it just outputs structured text saying ‘call this function with these arguments.’ The surrounding application actually executes that call, and feeds the real result back into the model’s context so it can write a final answer grounded in real, live data instead of a guess.

💡
Think of it like: a manager who can’t personally check the live stock price, but knows exactly who to ask — delegates the lookup, waits for the answer, then reports back using the real number.
Lesson 30

AI Agent: A Loop, Not Just an Answer

An agent doesn’t just answer once — it repeatedly plans, acts, and checks its own progress until the goal is met.

User
Goal
AI Agent
Reason / Plan
Tools
Observe Result
Memoryremember what happened
Goal Complete?no &rarr; loop again
↻ loops through plan &rarr; act &rarr; observe until the goal check passes, then returns the Final Answer

A single LLM call is a one-shot response. An agent wraps that call in a loop: it plans a next step, uses a tool, observes what happened, updates its memory, and checks whether the overall goal is done — repeating until it is (or it gives up). This is what lets an LLM tackle multi-step tasks instead of only answering single questions.

💡
Think of it like: a project manager who doesn’t just give one instruction and walk away — they plan, delegate, check the result, adjust the plan, and repeat until the project is actually finished.
Lesson MASTER

The Complete Modern LLM Application

Everything in this course is a piece of a bigger machine: an orchestrator around a raw LLM, giving it memory, tools, and fresh knowledge.

A raw LLM only does one thing well: predict the next token. Every production AI application wraps that core with an orchestrator that decides, per request, whether to fetch context via RAG, call out to tools/APIs, consult memory from earlier in the conversation, or just answer directly from the model — then assembles all of it into a final response. Everything you just learned, from tokenization up through agents, is one layer of this stack.

💡
Think of it like: a restaurant kitchen — the LLM is the chef, but the chef alone isn’t the restaurant. Orders (orchestrator), a pantry of fresh ingredients (RAG), specialized equipment (tools), and notes on regular customers (memory) all have to work together to actually serve a meal.
