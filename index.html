Glossary
A quick reference for important concepts used throughout the lessons. Each term includes a simple explanation and an everyday analogy.
Artificial Intelligence (AI)
Artificial Intelligence is the broad field of building machines or software that can perform tasks that normally require human-like intelligence, such as understanding language, recognizing patterns, making predictions, or solving problems.

Analogy: Think of AI as the entire school. Machine Learning, Deep Learning, NLP, and LLMs are different subjects taught inside that school.
Machine Learning (ML)
Machine Learning is a way of building systems that learn patterns from examples instead of being explicitly programmed with every rule. During training, the model adjusts its internal parameters so that its predictions become better over time.

Analogy: Instead of giving a student a rule for recognizing every type of fruit, you show them hundreds of examples and let them discover the patterns themselves.
Neural Network
A neural network is a mathematical model made of layers of interconnected units that transform numbers. Each layer learns useful patterns from the previous layer, allowing the network to represent increasingly complex relationships.

Analogy: Imagine an assembly line. Each worker performs a small transformation, and after many workers process the material, you get a much more useful final product.
Deep Learning
Deep Learning is Machine Learning based on neural networks with many layers. These layers allow the model to automatically learn increasingly complex representations from data.

Analogy: If a simple neural network is a few workers on an assembly line, deep learning is a huge factory with many stages, each discovering more complex patterns.
NLP
NLP stands for Natural Language Processing. It is the field of AI concerned with processing and understanding human language. NLP systems can perform tasks such as translation, summarization, classification, question answering, and text generation.

Analogy: Think of NLP as a translator between humans who communicate using language and computers that ultimately operate on numbers.
Token
A token is a small piece of text that a language model processes as one unit. Depending on the tokenizer, a token can be a complete word, part of a word, punctuation, whitespace, or another text fragment.

The model does not directly process a sentence as human-readable words. The sentence is first broken into tokens and then converted into numerical representations.

Analogy: Think of a sentence as a LEGO structure. Tokens are the individual LEGO pieces used to build that structure.
Tokenization
Tokenization is the process of breaking text into tokens that a language model can process. A tokenizer uses a fixed vocabulary and rules for deciding how text should be split.

Common words may remain as one token, while rare or long words may be split into multiple subword tokens.

Analogy: Imagine cutting a sentence into LEGO pieces. Familiar shapes can remain as large pieces, while unusual shapes are broken into smaller pieces that the system already knows.
Token ID
A Token ID is the integer assigned to a token in a model's vocabulary. The ID itself has no inherent meaning; it simply acts as an index that lets the model look up the corresponding learned representation.

For example, a vocabulary might assign one number to "cat" and another number to "dog."

Analogy: Think of a library card catalog. The book title has meaning to you, while its catalog number simply tells the librarian where to find it.
Vocabulary
A vocabulary is the collection of tokens that a tokenizer and language model know how to represent. Every token in the vocabulary has an associated token ID.

Analogy: Think of it as the dictionary of LEGO pieces available to the model. The model can only directly use pieces that exist in its vocabulary.
Embedding
An embedding is a learned vector of numbers used to represent information such as a token. Instead of treating a token ID as just an arbitrary number, the model looks up a vector containing many learned numerical values.

These representations allow the model to work with relationships between tokens in a continuous numerical space.

Analogy: Imagine giving every word a location on a giant map. Words with related meanings tend to occupy related regions of the map.
Vector
A vector is an ordered collection of numbers. In machine learning, vectors are used to represent information numerically so mathematical operations can be performed on it.

An embedding is one important example of a vector.

Analogy: Think of a vector as a set of coordinates describing where something is located on a mathematical map.
Semantic Vector Space
A semantic vector space is a mathematical space where objects such as words or sentences are represented as vectors. The learned positions can capture aspects of meaning and relationships between concepts.

Similar concepts tend to have representations that are related or close according to a chosen similarity measure.

Analogy: Imagine a giant map of concepts. "Doctor" might appear near "hospital," while "banana" might appear much closer to "fruit."
Positional Information
Positional information tells the Transformer where each token occurs in the sequence. This is important because attention by itself does not inherently tell the model whether a token came first, second, or last.

Position information allows the model to distinguish between sequences where the same tokens appear in different orders.

Analogy: Imagine giving someone a pile of numbered puzzle pieces. The pieces alone are not enough; the numbers tell you where each piece belongs.
Logits
Logits are the raw, unnormalized scores produced by the model for possible next tokens. Every candidate token receives a score, and these scores are later converted into probabilities.

A higher logit means the model is currently assigning a stronger score to that candidate relative to candidates with lower scores.

Analogy: Imagine judges giving contestants raw marks before those marks are converted into percentages or rankings. The raw marks are the logits.
Softmax
Softmax converts a collection of raw scores into a probability distribution. The resulting probabilities are non-negative and sum to 1.

In a language model, softmax can turn the logits for thousands of possible next tokens into probabilities that indicate how strongly each candidate is preferred.

Analogy: Imagine several students receiving different raw marks. Softmax converts those scores into percentages representing each student's share of the total probability.
Attention
Attention is a mechanism that allows a token to gather information from other tokens in the sequence. Instead of treating every token equally, the model calculates which other tokens are more relevant to the current token.

This allows meaning to change based on context.

Analogy: Imagine reading a sentence and deciding which previous words you should pay attention to in order to understand the meaning of the current word.
Self-Attention
Self-attention is attention where the tokens in a sequence attend to other tokens from the same sequence. Each token can use information from other relevant tokens to create a more context-aware representation.

Analogy: Imagine a group discussion where every person can listen to everyone else before forming their own understanding of the topic.
Query / Key / Value
Query, Key, and Value are three learned representations used by attention.

Query: What information am I looking for?
Key: What kind of information do I represent?
Value: What information can I provide?

The model compares a Query with Keys to determine which Values should receive more attention.

Analogy: Think of a library. Your question is the Query, the labels on the books are the Keys, and the actual information inside the books is the Value.
Attention Score
An attention score measures how relevant one token is to another token during the attention calculation. Higher scores indicate stronger relationships before the scores are normalized into attention weights.

Analogy: Imagine asking a group of people for help. The attention score is like deciding how relevant each person's answer is to your particular question.
Attention Weight
An attention weight is the normalized importance assigned to another token during attention. These weights determine how much information from each token contributes to the resulting representation.

Analogy: If five people give you advice, you might trust one person's answer more than the others. The amount of trust given to each person is like an attention weight.
Multi-Head Attention
Multi-Head Attention runs multiple attention mechanisms in parallel. Different heads can learn to focus on different relationships or patterns within the same sequence. Their outputs are then combined.

Analogy: Imagine several detectives investigating the same case. One studies people, another studies locations, another studies time, and another searches for hidden connections. Together they produce a richer understanding.
Transformer
A Transformer is a neural network architecture built around attention mechanisms. It processes sequences by allowing tokens to interact with one another and then transforms those representations through additional neural network layers.

Transformers became the foundation for modern large language models because they can capture relationships between tokens effectively and process many tokens in parallel during training.

Analogy: Think of a Transformer as a sophisticated team workshop where every team member can communicate with the others, analyze the information, and refine their understanding repeatedly.
Feed-Forward Network (FFN)
A Feed-Forward Network is the part of a Transformer block that processes each token's representation independently after attention has mixed contextual information between tokens.

It typically expands the representation into a larger space, applies a non-linear activation, and then projects it back down.

Analogy: Attention is like a group discussion where everyone exchanges information. The FFN is like each person going back to their own desk to think deeply about what they just learned.
Residual Connection
A residual connection adds a layer's original input back to its transformed output. This creates a shortcut through the network and allows a layer to focus on learning the changes it needs to make rather than rebuilding the entire representation from scratch.

Residual connections also help information and gradients travel through deep networks.

Analogy: Imagine editing a document. Instead of rewriting the entire document, you keep the original version and simply apply the necessary changes on top of it.
Transformer Block
A Transformer block is one repeated unit of a Transformer. It typically contains an attention mechanism, a feed-forward network, residual connections, and normalization components.

Many such blocks are stacked together to create a deep Transformer model.

Analogy: Think of one Transformer block as one floor of a building. Each floor performs similar kinds of work, and stacking many floors creates the complete building.
Transformer Stack
A Transformer stack is a sequence of Transformer blocks placed one after another. Each block receives the representations produced by the previous block and refines them further.

Analogy: Imagine sending a rough idea through several editors. The first editor improves the structure, the next improves the meaning, another improves the details, and so on.
Encoder
An encoder is the part of a Transformer architecture designed to build rich representations of an input sequence. It can generally use information from the entire input when creating those representations.

Analogy: Think of an encoder as someone reading an entire document first and building a detailed understanding of what it contains.
Decoder
A decoder generates an output sequence, typically one token at a time. In autoregressive language models, it uses causal attention so that a token cannot see future tokens that have not been generated yet.

Analogy: Think of a writer composing a story one sentence at a time while being allowed to see everything already written, but not the future pages.
Causal Mask
A causal mask prevents a token from attending to tokens that appear later in the sequence. This prevents the model from seeing future information when it is supposed to predict the next token.

Analogy: Imagine taking an exam where you can see the questions you've reached, but you are not allowed to look at the next page for the answers.
Next-Token Prediction
Next-token prediction is the fundamental training objective of many modern language models. Given the tokens that came before, the model learns to predict what token should come next.

For example, given "The sky is", the model might assign high probability to tokens such as "blue."

Analogy: Imagine someone finishing your sentences by guessing what word you are most likely to say next.
Autoregressive
Autoregressive generation means producing output one token at a time, where each newly generated token becomes part of the context used to generate the next token.

Analogy: Imagine telling a story one word at a time. After saying each word, you use everything you've said so far to decide what should come next.
Loss
Loss is a numerical measurement of how different the model's prediction is from the desired target. During training, the model uses this signal to determine how its parameters should be adjusted.

Lower loss generally means the model's predictions are closer to the training targets under that particular loss function.

Analogy: Think of loss as a "mistake meter" on a student's test. The larger the mistake, the larger the number on the meter.
Backpropagation
Backpropagation is the algorithmic process of calculating how the loss changes with respect to the model's parameters. It works backward through the network using the chain rule of calculus to calculate gradients.

Those gradients are then used by an optimization algorithm to update the model's parameters.

Analogy: Imagine a group project producing a bad result. You trace the problem backward through each decision to determine which changes would most improve the next attempt.
Gradient
A gradient tells us how much the loss would change if a model parameter changed slightly. During training, gradients provide the direction and magnitude of adjustment needed to reduce the loss.

Analogy: Imagine standing on a mountain and wanting to walk downhill. The gradient gives you information about the slope so you know which direction leads downward.
Weight / Parameter
A parameter is a learned numerical value inside a neural network. Weights are a major type of parameter. During training, these values are adjusted so the model becomes better at its task.

A modern LLM can contain millions, billions, or even hundreds of billions of learned parameters.

Analogy: Think of parameters as thousands or millions of tiny knobs inside a machine. Training repeatedly adjusts those knobs until the machine behaves better.
Pretraining
Pretraining is the initial large-scale training stage where a language model learns general patterns from a huge amount of text. Through this process, the model learns statistical relationships involving language, syntax, concepts, and information present in its training data.

Analogy: Think of pretraining as general school education. Before becoming a specialist, a student first develops a broad foundation across many subjects.
Fine-Tuning
Fine-tuning is additional training performed on an already pretrained model using a more focused dataset. It can teach the model to perform a particular task, follow a particular format, or behave in a particular way.

Analogy: Pretraining is general education; fine-tuning is specialization. A student first learns broadly and then trains specifically to become a doctor, engineer, teacher, or lawyer.
RLHF / Alignment
RLHF stands for Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback. It refers to techniques that use human preferences or evaluations to make model behavior more aligned with what people consider useful, appropriate, or desirable.

Alignment is the broader goal of making model behavior better match intended human goals and constraints.

Analogy: Imagine a teacher evaluating several answers and explaining not only which one is correct, but which answer is clearer, safer, more helpful, and more polite. The student gradually learns those preferences.
Training
Training is the process of adjusting a model's parameters using data so that it becomes better at a particular objective. The model repeatedly makes predictions, calculates loss, computes gradients, and updates its parameters.

Analogy: Training is practice. The model attempts a task repeatedly, receives feedback about its mistakes, and gradually improves.
Inference
Inference is the process of using an already-trained model to produce an output. During normal inference, the model's learned parameters are not updated; the model simply uses what it has already learned.

Analogy: Training is studying for an exam. Inference is taking the exam using what you learned.
RAG
RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. A RAG system retrieves relevant information from an external knowledge source and provides that information to an LLM as context before generating an answer.

A simplified flow is:
Question → Retrieve information → Add context → Generate answer

Analogy: Imagine a student taking an open-book exam. Instead of relying entirely on memory, the student searches the textbook for the relevant information and then uses it to answer.
Retrieval
Retrieval is the process of finding information that is relevant to a user's query from an external collection of documents or data.

In RAG systems, retrieval happens before generation so the LLM receives useful external context.

Analogy: Imagine asking a librarian a question. Before answering, the librarian searches the shelves and brings you the most relevant books or pages.
Context
Context is the information available to the model when it is generating a response. It can include the user's prompt, previous conversation, retrieved documents, system instructions, and other information supplied to the model.

Analogy: Imagine answering a question while being allowed to keep a set of notes on your desk. Those notes are your context.
Tool / Function Calling
Tool or function calling allows an LLM to request that an external program or tool perform a specific operation. The model does not necessarily perform the operation itself; it produces a structured request that the application can execute and then return the result to the model.

Examples include searching a database, checking weather, calling an API, calculating something, or sending a message.

Analogy: Imagine an employee who cannot directly access the company's database but can ask the database specialist to look something up and return the result.
AI Agent
An AI agent is a system that uses an LLM together with tools, instructions, state or memory, and an execution loop to accomplish a goal. Instead of producing only one answer, it can decide what action to take, use a tool, observe the result, and continue until the task is complete.

A simplified loop is:
Goal → Plan → Act → Observe → Decide → Act again

Analogy: A normal chatbot is like asking an employee a question and getting an answer. An AI agent is like giving an employee a task and allowing them to decide which tools and steps are needed to finish it.
LLM
LLM stands for Large Language Model. It is a neural network trained on large amounts of text to learn patterns in language and generate text. Modern LLMs are commonly built using Transformer architectures and are typically trained using next-token prediction.

Analogy: Think of an LLM as an extremely well-practiced language student that has seen an enormous number of examples and learned patterns about how language is used.

A neural network is just several simple transformations stacked on top of each other, layer by layer.

Hidden Layer 1
Hidden Layer 2
Hidden Layer 3

Each layer takes the numbers from the layer before it, multiplies them by learned weights, and passes the result forward. One layer alone isn’t very smart. 💡
Think of it like: an assembly line — each station does one small, simple operation, but after enough stations the raw material has become a finished product.

Lesson 06

Tokenization: Chopping Text into Pieces

Before a model can process text, it has to be split into small, consistent chunks called tokens.

“I love artificial intelligence”
tokenizer
Iloveartificialintelligence
Try it — live tokenizer
Illustrative demo — splits on whitespace and breaks long words into sub-pieces, roughly like a real tokenizer would.

A tokenizer doesn’t always split on whole words. Common words usually stay whole. Rare or long words get broken into smaller, familiar pieces (like art + ificial). This way, the model can represent any word — even ones it has never seen — using a small, fixed set of pieces.

💡
Think of it like: cutting a sentence into LEGO bricks — common shapes stay as one big brick, unusual shapes get broken into several small bricks the model already recognizes.
🐍 Python
text = "I love artificial intelligence"
tokens = tokenizer.encode(text)
# ['I', 'love', 'art', 'ificial', 'intelligence']
token_ids = [vocab[t] for t in tokens]
# [312, 891, 4021, 4022, 205]

Lesson 08

Embeddings: Giving Numbers Meaning

A token ID alone carries no meaning — embeddings convert it into a rich vector that captures meaning.

Token IDe.g. 4021
Embedding Layerlearned lookup table
High-Dim Vectore.g. 768 numbers
Semantic Representation

ID 4021 doesn’t tell the model anything about what “cat” means — it’s just a label. The embedding layer turns that ID into a vector of hundreds of numbers. This vector is learned during training, so words with similar meaning end up with similar vectors. This is the step where a token stops being a plain symbol and becomes a point in ‘meaning space.’

💡
Think of it like: translating a word into GPS coordinates on a map of meaning — the number itself looks arbitrary, but its position relative to other words is not.
🐍 Python
import torch.nn as nn

embedding = nn.Embedding(vocab_size=50000, embedding_dim=768)
vector = embedding(token_id)   # shape: [768]
# similar words -> similar vectors
cosine_similarity(embedding(cat_id), embedding(dog_id))  # high
cosine_similarity(embedding(cat_id), embedding(car_id))  # low

Lesson 10

Positional Information: Word Order Matters

“Dog bites man” and “man bites dog” use the same words — order changes everything.

The+ pos 1
dog+ pos 2
chased+ pos 3
the+ pos 4
cat+ pos 5

Embeddings tell the model what a word means, but not where it sits in the sentence. A Transformer looks at all tokens at the same time, not in reading order. So a positional signal gets added to each token’s embedding — this is how the model tells “dog chased cat” apart from “cat chased dog.”

💡
Think of it like: stamping every word with a timestamp before throwing all the words into a bag — you can dump them out in any order and still reconstruct the original sentence.

The Attention Mechanism

The single most important idea in modern LLMs — letting every token look at every other token.

Lesson 11

Why Attention? Context Changes Meaning

The word “it” is meaningless on its own — its meaning depends entirely on the rest of the sentence.

The animal didn’t cross the road because it was too tired.

“it” → animal  ✓
“it” → road  ✗
Try it — click a word

The animal didn’t cross the road because it was too tired.

Click “it” to see which word the model should pay attention to.

A model reading this sentence has to work out that “it” means the animal, not the road. Why? Because “tired” makes sense for a living thing, not a road. To do this, every word needs to look at every other word and judge how relevant it is. That mechanism is called attention — the single most important idea behind modern LLMs.

💡
Think of it like: a detective re-reading every earlier clue in a story before deciding what a pronoun really refers to.

Lesson 12

Self-Attention: Every Token Looks at Every Token

Self-attention lets each token gather context from every other token in the sequence, weighted by relevance.

All Token Reps
Querywhat am I looking for?
Keyswhat do others offer?
Attention Scoresrelevance
Attention Weightsnormalized
Context-Aware Output

For every token, the model asks a question — that’s the Query. It compares this question against what every other token has to offer — the Keys. This gives a relevance score for each token. Those scores become weights, and the token’s new representation becomes a blend of every other token, weighted by relevance. Now the token is ‘aware’ of its context, instead of standing alone.

💡
Think of it like: a group discussion where, before speaking, everyone briefly checks in with everyone else in the room to see whose input is most relevant to the current topic.

Lesson 13

Query, Key, Value — The Three Roles

Query asks “what am I looking for,” Key answers “here’s what I contain,” Value carries the actual content.

Input Representation
Query (Q)the question
Key (K)the label
Q × Kmatch score
Softmaxturn into weights
Weighted Values
Attention Output

Every token gets turned into three separate vectors, using three learned weight matrices. The Query is what this token is searching for. The Key is what each token has to offer. Matching Query against Key gives relevance scores. Those scores are then used to blend the Values — the actual content each token carries.

💡
Think of it like: a library search: your Query is your search terms, every book’s Key is its catalog tags, and once you find good matches, the Value is the actual book content you walk away with.

The Attention Formula, Step by Step

Behind the diagrams is one compact formula — here’s exactly what each piece does.

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax( QKT / √dk ) V
Q × Kᵀsimilarity scores
÷ √dₖkeep values stable
Softmaxturn into probabilities
× Vblend the values
Attention Output

QKT multiplies queries against keys to get raw similarity scores. Dividing by √dk just keeps those numbers from growing too large. Softmax turns the scores into clean probabilities that add up to 1. Multiplying by V then blends the actual content, based on those probabilities.

💡
Think of it like: a recipe with exactly four steps — each one boring on its own, but skip any single step and the whole dish fails.
🐍 Python
scores = Q @ K.transpose(-2, -1)      # QK^T
scores = scores / math.sqrt(d_k)      # scale
weights = softmax(scores, dim=-1)     # probabilities
output = weights @ V                  # weighted blend

The Feed-Forward Network

After attention mixes information between tokens, the feed-forward network processes each token individually to refine it.

Attention Output
Linear Layerexpand
Activationadd non-linearity
Linear Layercompress back

Attention is about tokens talking to each other. The FFN does the opposite: it takes each token’s representation and runs it through a small neural network, on its own, without looking at other tokens. This further refines what each token ‘knows.’ This pattern — mix, then think — repeats throughout the whole model.

💡
Think of it like: a team meeting (attention, everyone shares notes) followed by quiet, independent desk work (FFN, each person processes what they just heard on their own).

Encoder vs. Decoder

Transformers come in two flavors, depending on whether the goal is to understand text or to generate it.

Transformer
EncoderUnderstand / Represent

An encoder looks at the whole input at once and builds a rich representation of it. It’s good for classification, search, and understanding tasks. For every sentence in a huge training set, the model sees some words and tries to predict the next one. It compares its guess to the real answer, calculates a loss, and sends that error backward through every layer to update the weights. Do this across a trillion-plus tokens of text, and the model ends up absorbing a huge amount of knowledge and language structure — just as a side effect of getting better at predicting the next word.

Pretraining teaches the raw model language, facts, and reasoning patterns from huge amounts of text. Lesson 27

Training vs. Inference

Training is the one-time (expensive) process of learning weights. Inference is every single time you actually use the model.

TRAINING — happens once
Dataset
Loss
Backpropagation
Updated Weights
VS
INFERENCE — happens every chat
User Prompt
Fixed Weightsfrozen, unchanged
Forward Pass Only
Generated Answer

During training, the model’s weights keep changing as it learns from data. This happens once, or occasionally for updates, and it’s expensive. During inference — every time you chat with an LLM — the weights stay completely frozen. The model just runs a forward pass through those fixed weights to produce a response. Nothing is being learned from your specific conversation.

💡
Think of it like: the difference between years spent training for a marathon (training, you change and improve) and actually running race day (inference, you just execute what you already learned — no more improving mid-race).
